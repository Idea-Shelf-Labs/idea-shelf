# Qattara depression lake
**Claim:** Run as one coupled system, nuclear-heated MED, surplus-power RO, a solar-clad pipeline over the ridge, and hydro on the descent are designed to cut the net energy cost of filling a below-sea-level basin with freshwater.
**Status:** ready · test specified · lab card · unfinished experiment
**Terms (once):** **Qattara Depression** = the below-sea-level basin in northwestern Egypt (Matruh Governorate), Western Desert — the classic candidate site, not a locked one. **MED** = multi-effect distillation. **RO** = reverse osmosis. **Lift-to-drop** = how high the water must be pumped over the ridge compared with how far it can fall into the basin. **Corridor** = one coastal desal plant, one pipeline route, one receiving basin. **First build** = one reactor-class heat and power source, one corridor, one basin — not an inland sea on day one. **Seawater scheme** = historical proposals that admit Mediterranean seawater for hydropower and evaporation balance; ends hypersaline or as a salt pan. This idea is the other path.
Working figures and unknowns (labeled). Nothing below is a build commitment.
- **Basin size (Qattara candidate):** about **19,605 km²** lies below sea level. Floor averages roughly **60 m** below sea level; low point about **133 m** below sea level (second-lowest point in Africa after Lake Assal, Djibouti). Working figures from the Ball survey lineage (aneroid 1917; Walpole triangulation 1924–25; Ball, *The Geographical Journal*, 1927) (**established** for area and average floor depth; low point **published-contested** across surveys).
- **Distance to sea:** shortest Mediterranean approach about **56 km** (**established**); a studied canal route runs about **73–75 km** (**established**, OSTI 1980 and IAEA 1976). Published descriptions put the northern plateau at about **200 m** above sea level on average (**established**, Ragheb 2019), so a freshwater pipe faces a lift on that order; the exact crest and pump lift on a chosen route remain **unknown**.
- **Floor character:** salt pans, sabkha (salt marsh), dunes, and fech-fech (fine powdered sand). Steep escarpments on the northern and northwestern sides. Qara is the only permanent settlement in the depression; Siwa Oasis lies just to the west (**established**).
- **Not the seawater scheme:** Penck (~1912), Ball (1927 outline; 1933 power paper), later Bassler / Joint Venture Qattara studies, and related papers proposed admitting seawater so the drop could drive turbines while evaporation held a level (**established**). A nuclear-excavation canal concept (**213** underground devices in the Plowshare-era proposal; **established**) was examined and not adopted (**established**). Egypt's 2026 review, reported on **12 May 2026**, rejected seawater filling and also judged a desalinate-at-the-rim pipeline scenario not feasible; its findings, stated risks, and sources are under Prior review in §2 (**established**).
- **Hybrid architecture, in three ledgers:** the design is a hybrid, and its energy claims split into three separate accounts. **Thermal:** reactor heat that would otherwise be rejected drives MED; MED's own electricity use is only for pumps and auxiliaries, though taking the steam also costs the reactor some output (below). Nuclear waste heat reduces the marginal energy cost of thermal desalination — it is not free: taking the heat has an opportunity cost, needs equipment, and can affect reactor operation (**established** as design principle in IAEA nuclear-desalination guidance). How much usable heat, at what temperature, a chosen reactor here offers is **not found**. Temperature is the catch: low-temperature MED runs at a top brine temperature of about **65–70 °C** (**established**: makers and the literature give about 60–70 °C), while a reactor's condenser heat comes out at only about **30–40 °C** (**working assumption**, typical condenser range). So MED here takes extraction or back-pressure steam from the turbine, which costs some electricity. It is cheap heat, not free heat. MED on this card runs about **1–2.5 kWh/m³** electrical plus **230–390 kJ/kg** thermal, which is about **64–108 kWh** of heat per m³ (**published-contested**: published ranges vary by plant, design, and vintage; plain MED makers quote under 1 kWh/m³ electrical, MED with thermal vapor compression about 1.5–2.5; the heat figure is converted arithmetic). Hybrid nuclear MED+RO is itself published: an IAEA design example couples a 600 MWth HTGR's 310 MWth of waste heat to LT-MED plus preheated RO for about **2,182 m³/h**, about 52,000 m³/day (**established** as a published design study, not a built plant). That whole design is about 5% of one 1-million-m³/day plant pair (derived), so a large pair needs a much bigger heat and power source; the reactor size for this card is **not found**. **Electrical:** RO runs on surplus electricity when it is abundant — not as a continuous baseload load — at about **3–5.5 kWh/m³** (**published-contested**), alongside pumping and pipe losses. For scale, every 1 million m³/day of RO needs about **125–230 MW** of average electricity (derived: 1,000,000 m³ × 3–5.5 kWh/m³ ÷ 24 h). Because RO runs mainly on surplus power, its installed size must be larger than its average output; that share of hours is **not found**. The solar-clad corridor supplies part of that load where it runs. Illustrative only: a 70 km × 10 m clad strip is 0.7 km², which at 200 W/m² is about **140 MWp** of nameplate (**working assumption**: illustrative inputs, named here — the result is a derived nameplate, not an annual yield — actual output also depends on packing, orientation, and shading — and established inputs do not make the conclusion established). Annual solar yield on a real corridor is **not found**. **Hydraulic:** one reference geometry is used throughout this card. Product water starts at sea level (0 m), is pumped up to a ridge crest at **200 m**, and flows down to a lake surface at **60 m below sea level** (**working assumption**: 200 m is the published average plateau height, since the crest on a real route is **not found**; −60 m is the average floor depth). That gives a lift of **200 m**, a drop of **260 m**, and a net drop of **60 m**. One meter of head is worth about **0.002725 kWh/m³** (E = ρgh, with ρ = 1,000 kg/m³ and g = 9.81 m/s²; **established** as physics), so the ideal lift is about **0.55 kWh/m³**, the ideal recovery about **0.71 kWh/m³**, and the ideal net gain about **0.16 kWh/m³** (**established** as arithmetic from this geometry). Real machines lose energy. With a pump-plus-turbine round trip of **80–85%**, split evenly between pump and turbine (**working assumption**), the lift takes about **0.59–0.61 kWh/m³** of electricity and the drop returns about **0.63–0.65 kWh/m³**, a net gain of about **0.02–0.06 kWh/m³** before pipe friction (**working assumption**, derived from that geometry and round trip). Pipe friction on a real route is **not found**; it depends on pipe size and flow. The bound is simple: about **8–21 m** of friction head over the whole 56–80 km route, roughly **0.1–0.4 m per km**, would erase that net gain (derived from the same inputs). A lake of 180–490 km² would likely pool below the average floor depth, which adds drop (each extra 10 m adds about 0.027 kWh/m³ ideal), but that lake surface elevation is **not found**, so the card does not count it. In plain words: once losses are counted, the drop pays back most or all of the lift and at best leaves a few hundredths of a kWh per m³. That is under about 2% of RO's 3–5.5 kWh/m³ (derived), so the drop does not pay for desalination. The real savings come from three other places: reactor waste heat for MED, surplus electricity for RO, and dispatch that runs each machine when its energy is cheapest. Whole-system energy-neutral including desalination is **not claimed**, and gravity cannot deliver it. The test target is low purchased power instead: the plant should buy little or no outside electricity because the corridor's own solar and the reactor's surplus cover the load, which the coupled corridor model has to show; it is not a result. **No measured pump-kWh vs turbine-kWh ratio or pipe loss figure is in this card.**
- **Dispatch and feed warming:** the reactor runs at steady output. Around the solar midday, when grid power is cheap, it sends more steam to MED; in the evening it sends more to the grid. The ridge-top dam and MED buffering smooth the daily swings. A continuous dispatch optimizer balances electricity, MED, and RO hour by hour, so part of the savings comes from how the plant is run, not only from its hardware (**working assumption**). Spare low-grade heat can also pre-warm the RO feed from about **20 °C** to the low **30s °C** (**working assumption**: intake temperature varies by season). Membrane makers' rule of thumb is about **3%** more water through the membrane per °C (**established**), which here might mean **5–10%** less RO energy (**working assumption**, not modeled). The trade-offs: salt passage rises faster, about **6%** per °C by the same rule of thumb (**established**), and membranes have a feed temperature limit of about **35–45 °C** (45 °C is one major maker's stated limit, **established**; the lower end is a **working assumption** for long membrane life).
- **Lake goal and desal sizing:** the goal is a lake about the size of Lake Tahoe, about **490 km²** (**established** as Tahoe's area). Holding that size steady takes about **2.7 million m³/day** just to replace evaporation (**working assumption**, derived: 490 km² × the 5,500 m³/day per km² rate below), and more if the bed seeps or water is drawn off. For scale, Taweelah RO in Abu Dhabi makes about **909,000 m³/day**, Sorek 2 RO in Israel about **670,000 m³/day** installed, Jubail's MED plant in Saudi Arabia about **800,000 m³/day**, and the Ras Al Khair thermal-plus-RO hybrid about **1,036,000 m³/day** (**established**). One large MED+RO plant pair is taken here as about **1.0–1.5 million m³/day** of average delivered water (**working assumption**, from those reference sizes; installed size would be larger, see Electrical), so the goal needs about **2–3** such pairs (**working assumption**, derived: 2.7 million ÷ 1.0–1.5 million). The first step is one pair and a lake of about **180–270 km²** (**working assumption**, derived: 1.0–1.5 million m³/day ÷ 5,500 m³/day per km², with no seepage and nothing drawn off). For comparison: Shasta Lake is about **120 km²**, Lake Tahoe about **490 km²**, and Lake Mead about **640 km²** at full pool (**established**). The MED vs RO split, brine volume, and reactor thermal and electric rating are still **not found**.
- **Co-siting:** the desal plants sit at the reactor site and share its seawater intake and outfall (**working assumption**). Big plants need hectares, not square kilometers: Sorek 2 sits on about **9.4 ha** (**established**).
- **Hybrid desal pilots (not this plant's design):** NF pretreatment let a **20 m³/day** MSF pilot run at **130 °C** top brine temperature for 1,200 hours without scale additive, with recovery up to **70%** versus about **35%** for conventional MSF (**established** as a published pilot result). A GOR of 13 for that pilot was **not found** — other NF-MSF configurations report different GORs, and published NF energy figures vary (**published-contested**). MED-AD hybrids roughly doubled to tripled distillate in modeled and lab/prototype work by extending the cold end from about **40 °C** toward **5–10 °C** (**published-contested**: modeled and prototype results, not commercial scale). Neither hybrid is a Qattara plant design or cost.
- **Freshwater retention:** whether a filled freshwater surface leaks into saline aquifers, how fast evaporation removes inventory, and whether a useful lake level can be held are **unknown**. Ball's 1933 estimate put depression-lake evaporation at **4–6 mm/day** (common secondary range, Ball 1933, for the seawater scheme). Freshwater evaporates faster than brine: Ball's figures imply about **5.5 mm/day** for freshwater, falling by about **2.7 mm/day** toward near-saturated brine (**published** figures, via a Texas Water Development Board summary of Ball); a measured freshwater-lake evaporation and leakage rate for this basin was **not found**. Pouring into often-salty groundwater as a sweet aquifer bank was considered and **left off** on purpose. Lithium or other mineral grades in the salt pans were **not found** in the studies surveyed, so the pans are not presented as a mineral opportunity; published groundwater salinities in the Moghra aquifer range from brackish near the eastern margins to highly saline toward the depression (**published-contested**: primary studies report widely varying local values); extreme sabkha values were **not found** in a primary source opened here. The retention unknown is practical, not abstract: the floor rests predominantly on the Lower Miocene Moghra Formation — sand, sandstone, and clay layers (**established**) — whose permeability connects to the Moghra aquifer already being pumped for large-scale reclamation northeast of the depression (**established**), so a freshwater surface may not stay fresh or isolated. Salt is the other half of the problem: freshwater sitting on sabkha and salt pans dissolves rock salt from the floor, so the lake grows saltier over time unless the bed is lined or has engineered barriers (**working assumption**: the dissolving is basic chemistry, the rate here is unknown). This is the retention mechanism that must be tested. Long-term retention is not modeled on this card (**not found**). The scale of that evaporation is not small: at Ball's **5.5 mm/day** freshwater rate (**published** figure above; a rate measured for this basin was **not found**), each **1 km²** of lake loses about **5,500 m³/day**, and **100 km²** about **550,000 m³/day** (**established** as arithmetic from that rate). What the receiving basin is for — managed reservoir, habitat, agriculture supply, storage, or the lake itself — is an open, scored choice that sets how much of that loss matters; this card does not pick one.
- **Money and rights:** no Qattara capex, OPEX, water price, land or corridor right-of-way cost, or interconnection figure (**not found**). Simulation-only cost estimates exist for comparable plants: a CSP hybrid RO-MED simulation (7,600 m³/day, brackish feed, California) modeled water at **$0.45/m³** versus **$0.75/m³** for standalone MED (Weiner et al., 2015/2016), and a 5,000 m³/day solar-thermal MSF simulation for Mersa Matruh, Egypt found CSP-RO competitive with fossil-fired desalination while solar MSF cost about three times more (Hassabou et al., 2013) (**published-contested**: simulation results under each study's assumptions; transferring them to Qattara is a **working assumption**). A Qattara corridor cost is still **not found**. Who can refuse the corridor (coastal intake, pipe route, basin rim, petroleum overlap) is not mapped.
## 1. The problem
Picture a freshwater lake about the size of Lake Tahoe, about 490 km², with a green, planted edge, in a part of the Western Desert that today is salt pans, sabkha, and sand. The first step is smaller: one plant pair holding a lake of about 180–270 km², if evaporation, seepage, and salt allow it.
Arid basins sit below sea level within tens of kilometers of a salt sea. Historical engineering answered that geometry by admitting seawater: turbines on the drop, evaporation holding a level, and a hypersaline lake or salt pan as the end state. That path was rejected for Qattara in the May 2026 review on groundwater salt, soil, habitat, petroleum overlap, and cost grounds. The idea has been officially studied beyond seawater, too: the same review judged desalination options not feasible, including desalinating at the rim and piping the water out, and favored not using the depression as a reservoir at all (§2).
Freshwater is the scarce good inland. Making it at the coast and moving it over an escarpment costs energy. Without recovery on the descent, the lift is a pure loss; with it, most of the lift comes back, but desalination still costs energy. Without a scored brine and intake plan, coastal desal is an unfinished plant.
## 2. The insight
This is worth testing because the bet is not that gravity is free. It is that reactor waste heat for MED, cheap midday surplus power for RO, a solar-clad lift, and hour-by-hour dispatch could make freshwater cheaper than is usually assumed for a project like this, with the drop paying back most of the pumping. The limits come with it: the drop does not pay for desalination, whole-system energy-neutral including desalination is not claimed, and the test target is low purchased power, meaning the plant buys little or no outside electricity, which the coupled corridor model has to show.
Saltwater flooding is out: Egypt's May 2026 Cabinet review rejected filling the depression with Mediterranean seawater. Use the depression as a **freshwater** receiving volume, not as a seawater hydropower sink. Desalinate at the coast as a hybrid: reactor waste heat drives MED (its own electricity goes only to pumps and auxiliaries), and surplus electricity drives RO when it is abundant. Lift through solar-clad pipelines that generate along the corridor. Recover what the drop allows through hydro into the basin. A held freshwater surface is worth scoring against the rejected seawater alternative and against doing nothing.
Qattara is the classic geometry people mean. The site stays whichever basin has the best lift-to-drop, so hydro pays back as much of the pump as possible.
**Prior review:** Egypt has studied this. A ministerial committee formed under decree **3433** of **2016** assessed **five** development scenarios for the depression, and the Cabinet Media Center published the findings on **12 May 2026**. The committee rejected filling the depression with Mediterranean seawater, judged scenarios 1, 2, 3, and 5 not feasible, and favored scenario 4: not using the depression as a water reservoir (one outlet words it as a seawater reservoir). Scenarios 1 to 3 used all or part of the depression to store water and make power, two of them with desalination for farming. Scenario 5 is the closest to this card: desalinate seawater using the height difference at the rim, pipe the water straight to development uses, and use the salts from desalination in industry (**established**; see Appendix). The stated risks, framed mostly around seawater filling, were salty water seeping into groundwater and nearby wells, saltier soil, harm to desert ecosystems including Siwa Oasis, overlap with **35** oil production development areas and **8** exploration areas, relocating oil and gas pipelines, mineral deposits (bentonite, and carbonaceous shale at **6** sites), the high cost of a canal from the sea, and hydropower costing more than other power sources (**established**). Why scenario 5 in particular was judged not feasible is **not found** in the published findings. This card asks a different question: a freshwater lake rather than seawater, reactor waste heat for MED, RO on surplus power, hour-by-hour dispatch, and a basin bed that is lined or tested before any fill. It is still a reservoir in the plain sense the favored scenario avoids, and it still faces the seepage, Siwa, oil and gas, and cost risks. This card does not claim to overturn the review.
**Serious rival:** coastal desalination piped directly to farms and towns, with no depression lift or lake. This proposal differs by spending lift energy, most of it recovered on the descent, to create a held inland freshwater lake instead of only delivering water at the coast.
## 3. The proposal
### Coastal desal
One nuclear-driven hybrid plant on the Mediterranean: MED on reactor waste heat, RO on surplus electricity when it is there. Waste heat is not free — taking it has an opportunity cost, needs equipment, and can affect reactor operation — but it reduces the marginal energy cost of the thermal share. Product water is freshwater. Brine disposal, intake ecology, and plant siting are first-class design, not footnotes. The desal plants sit at the reactor site and share its seawater intake and outfall (**working assumption**). The reactor runs steady and shifts steam between MED and the grid through the day, with a dispatch optimizer balancing power, MED, and RO.
### Solar-clad lift
Pipelines carry product water over the escarpment. Solar cladding helps supply the lift and RO load. Exact route, diameter, lift height, and solar fraction are unknown. A small dam and reservoir at the ridge crest can turn that lift into pumped storage paired with solar: pump product water up when solar power is abundant, hold it — perhaps a week (**working assumption**; no source found verifies that duration) — and release it through the descent turbines when power is most needed. A published pumped-storage variant for this depression places an upper site at about **215 m** on the rim, with about **275 m** of drop to a lake at 60 m below sea level (**established** as published proposal figures, Ragheb 2019, written for the seawater scheme; applying them to freshwater flows is a **working assumption**). This card's energy ledger uses its own 200 m crest and 260 m drop, not these figures. Ridge storage shifts energy in time at a round-trip loss; it does not add energy.
### Hydro into the basin
On the descent into the below-sea-level floor, turbines recover head. The receiving volume is a depression lake of desalinated freshwater — local humidity, agriculture, and wildlife as possible end uses once water quality and retention are shown. Not Mediterranean brine.
### Staging
First build: one reactor-class source, one corridor, one basin. That first step is one large MED+RO plant pair holding a lake of about 180–270 km². The goal is a lake about Lake Tahoe's size, about 490 km², with two to three plant pairs. Wild end-state of a large inland freshwater frontier stays on the shelf as aspiration. Honest measurement starts at corridor scale.
### What this is not
- Not flooding Qattara (or any basin) with seawater.
- Not Ball/Bassler seawater hydropower revived under a freshwater name.
- Not a claim that groundwater refill creates a sweet aquifer bank in salty formations.
- Not a measured energy-neutral ratio, and not a claim that the system is energy-neutral.
- Not a locked site. Qattara is the classic candidate.
## 4. Why this
Seawater fill uses the drop but destroys the freshwater use-case and was rejected for Qattara on environmental and petroleum grounds. Coastal desal without hydro recovery wastes the basin’s head. Dry inland solar or wind without a water product does not create a lake. This proposal ties desal, lift, and drop into one scored corridor so the depression’s depth works for freshwater rather than against it.
## 5. How you'd know
After a first-corridor design score (and, if built, after steady product water reaches a held level):
- Pump work, pipe losses, desal specific energy, and turbine recovery are measured on the same water mass; net energy per cubic meter is published
- Product water in the basin stays within a stated salinity and contaminant band
- Leakage to groundwater and evaporation losses are measured, not assumed zero
- Brine and intake impacts at the coast stay inside the plant’s design limits
- Petroleum, settlement, and habitat constraints on the chosen corridor are explicit accepts or refuses — not surprises after fill
Megawatts of desal nameplate with no lift-to-drop score is a weak result.
## 6. First test
One corridor paper-and-field score before any fill commitment. Match plant, pipe, and basin to one candidate depression (Qattara or better lift-to-drop). The first, decisive test is a coupled model; the seven scored things follow it.
1. **Coupled corridor model (decisive, first).** One spreadsheet, hourly over a full year, for a single corridor: MED output from the reactor's usable waste heat (heat temperature, thermal MW available, MED performance, operating hours); RO on surplus electricity only; the dispatch rule (steady reactor, more steam to MED at the solar midday, more to the grid in the evening); pipe friction and pump and turbine efficiency on the chosen route; solar annual yield (not nameplate) against annual pumping plus RO consumption, with the storage or grid exchange needed to close the gaps; and a water balance for the receiving basin (evaporation, leakage, make-up flow) at a stated size and stated purpose. Run sensitivity ranges on every input that is a range on this card. If the three ledgers do not close on the same water in the model, nothing else on this list rescues the route.
2. **Lift-to-drop.** Pump kWh, pipe loss, and turbine kWh on the same mass balance. If hydro cannot pay back most of the pump once friction is counted, the route fails this test.
3. **Freshwater, not seawater.** Product is desalinated water. No Mediterranean admit.
4. **Brine and intake.** Disposal path and intake ecology are designed and costed, not deferred.
5. **Retention.** Evaporation and leakage estimates for that basin floor, plus how fast freshwater dissolves salt from the sabkha and salt-pan floor, with and without a liner or barrier; salty-aquifer bank claim stays off unless geology seals or stratifies. Pass only if modeled and field-tested seepage, including salt the lake has dissolved, does not raise salinity in nearby wells or groundwater; fail otherwise.
6. **Site unlock.** Compare at least one alternate basin on lift-to-drop. Qattara wins only if the numbers say so.
7. **Overlap and ecosystems.** Pass only if the corridor and lake footprint avoid, or are cleared to use, the oil production and exploration areas, the oil and gas pipelines, and the mineral sites the 2026 review named, and an ecology study shows no harm to Siwa Oasis, its springs and wetlands, or nearby desert habitat; fail on any one. Settlements (Qara, Siwa adjacency) can still refuse the corridor.
8. **Scale honesty.** First build is one reactor, one corridor, one basin. Inland-sea rhetoric is not the test.

## 8. Why it might not work
- Desal energy dominates, and pipe friction turns the small gravity gain into a net cost, so the lift adds energy on top of desalination and the plant buys far more outside electricity than the low purchased power target allows.
- Freshwater leaks into saline aquifers or evaporates faster than the plant can replace at acceptable cost.
- Freshwater dissolves salt from the floor and the lake turns brackish, or that salty water seeps into groundwater and nearby wells, the first risk the 2026 review named, unless lining or barriers work at a cost the project can carry.
- Pipe friction and pump and turbine losses eat the small net gravity advantage, so hydro only offsets part of the lift.
- Brine or intake ecology fails coastal permitting.
- Oil and gas areas and pipelines, settlements, or harm to Siwa and desert habitat block the only workable pipe route or lake footprint.
- An alternate basin beats Qattara, and Qattara-locked rhetoric fights the better geometry.
- Cost of nuclear desal plus long pressure pipe exceeds alternatives that deliver water without a depression lake.
- The project drifts back into seawater admit under a hydropower excuse — the path already rejected in May 2026.
- Scale jumps to “inland sea” before one corridor is scored.
Wrong in one line: the corridor cannot show lift-to-drop and brine that support a held freshwater basin.
## 9. Stress-test
- **“Near energy-neutral.”** Not claimed. Desalination needs about 1–2.5 kWh/m³ of electricity plus heat for MED and 3–5.5 kWh/m³ for RO, while the drop returns at most about 0.06 kWh/m³ net, so gravity cannot make the system energy-neutral. The test target is low purchased power instead: the plant should buy little or no outside electricity because the corridor's solar and the reactor's surplus cover the load. The coupled corridor model has to show that; if purchased electricity cannot be kept to the surplus the design assumes, the target fails for that design.
- **“A week of ridge storage.”** Working assumption, not a sourced duration. If the crest reservoir is too small to hold useful energy, or evaporation and leakage drain it before the release window, the pumped-storage claim fails and the reservoir is only a surge tank.
- **“Flood Qattara.”** False for this idea. Seawater admit is the historical scheme; May 2026 rejected it. This path is coastal freshwater only.
- **Ball 125–200 MW / Bassler ~5,800 MW.** Secondary accounts put Ball-scale power near **125–200 MW** (**established**, Ball's preliminary calculations) and one Bassler-era concept near **5,800 MW** (base plant plus pumped storage; **published-contested**, published stage figures do not fully agree). Those figures describe the rejected seawater path, not this proposal's power budget. Freshwater recovery here will be smaller: at the same flow and head freshwater yields slightly less power than seawater, and this scheme recovers only on desalinated product flow after paying desal and lift (**working assumption**, derived from the density difference and the cost order above).
- **Evaporation at lake scale.** At Ball's **5.5 mm/day** freshwater rate (**published** figure; measured for this basin **not found**), **100 km²** of held freshwater loses about **550,000 m³/day** to evaporation (**established** as arithmetic from that rate). If make-up flow at the chosen lake size and purpose costs more energy than the corridor saves, the held-lake version fails and only the delivered-water version remains.
- **Groundwater refill as sweet storage.** Left off on purpose. Salty formations do not reliably become a useful freshwater bank.
- **Qattara is required.** False. Classic candidate. Site = best lift-to-drop.
- **19,605 km² is the build.** Basin area below sea level is geography. First build is one corridor, one basin slice — not the whole floor.
- **“Egypt's 2026 review already rejected this.”** Partly true. It rejected seawater filling, judged a rim-desal pipeline scenario (scenario 5) not feasible for reasons not published, and favored not using the depression as a reservoir at all. This design differs in what goes into the basin and how desalination is powered: a freshwater lake, reactor waste heat for MED, RO on surplus power with hour-by-hour dispatch, and a bed lined or tested before fill. Those differences do not clear anything by themselves. The design still has to pass the same risks the review named: seepage into groundwater and wells, harm to Siwa and desert ecosystems, overlap with oil and gas areas and pipelines, and cost. If it cannot, the review's conclusion holds for this design too.
- **“Fill it for sea-level drawdown.”** The rejected seawater scheme in modern dress. Impounding a chosen intermediate, partial-fill volume of **361 km³** of seawater equals about **1 mm** of global sea-level drawdown by ocean-area arithmetic (**established** as arithmetic, from ocean area and impounded volume). The depression's capacity to sea level is roughly **1,200–1,340 km³** (**published-contested** across the Ball-lineage fill table at 1,213 km³ and later DEM estimates at about 1,340 km³), so 361 km³ is a partial volume, not the basin's volume. Egypt's May 2026 review rejected seawater filling. This freshwater proposal's relevant metric is annual freshwater throughput: about 2.7 million m³/day at the Tahoe-size goal, or roughly 1 km³ a year (**working assumption**, derived from the sizing above).
- **“This needs a national corridor.”** No. The goal is a Tahoe-size lake, about 490 km², held by two to three large plant pairs at one reactor site. That is about 2.5% of the 19,605 km² below sea level (**established** as arithmetic). The first step is one pair and about 180–270 km². If one pair cannot hold its lake against evaporation, seepage, and salt, the bigger goal fails with it.
- **“The lake will make it rain.”** Not claimed. A lake this size, with planting at its edge, should raise humidity and dew near the shore and might nudge rain slightly downwind; dry, sinking desert air limits more than that. It is a side effect to measure, not a benefit to count.
