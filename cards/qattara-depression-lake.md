# Qattara depression lake
**Claim:** Run as one coupled system, nuclear-heated MED, surplus-power RO, a solar-clad pipeline over the ridge, and hydro on the descent are designed to cut the net energy cost of filling a below-sea-level basin with freshwater.
**Status:** ready · test specified · lab card · unfinished experiment
**Terms (once):** **Qattara Depression** = the below-sea-level basin in northwestern Egypt (Matruh Governorate), Western Desert — the classic candidate site, not a locked one. **MED** = multi-effect distillation. **RO** = reverse osmosis. **Lift-to-drop** = how high the water must be pumped over the ridge compared with how far it can fall into the basin. **Corridor** = one coastal desal plant, one pipeline route, one receiving basin. **First build** = one reactor-class heat and power source, one corridor, one basin — not an inland sea on day one. **Seawater scheme** = historical proposals that admit Mediterranean seawater for hydropower and evaporation balance; ends hypersaline or as a salt pan. This idea is the other path.
Working figures and unknowns (labeled). Nothing below is a build commitment.
- **Basin size (Qattara candidate):** about **19,605 km²** lies below sea level. Floor averages roughly **60 m** below sea level; low point about **133 m** below sea level (second-lowest point in Africa after Lake Assal, Djibouti). Working figures from the Ball survey lineage (aneroid 1917; Walpole triangulation 1924–25; Ball, *The Geographical Journal*, 1927) (**established** for area and average floor depth; low point **published-contested** across surveys).
- **Distance to sea:** shortest Mediterranean approach about **56 km** (**established**); a studied canal route runs about **73–75 km** (**established**, OSTI 1980 and IAEA 1976). Published descriptions put the northern plateau at about **200 m** above sea level on average (**established**, Ragheb 2019). The DEM puts the crest of the shortest gap, about **55 km** on the east side of the basin, at about **148 m** (derived from Copernicus GLO-30). The illustrative route on this card lifts water to about **180–190 m** (see Hydraulic below).
- **Floor character:** salt pans, sabkha (salt marsh), dunes, and fech-fech (fine powdered sand). Steep escarpments on the northern and northwestern sides. Qara is the only permanent settlement in the depression; Siwa Oasis lies just to the west (**established**).
- **Not the seawater scheme:** Penck (~1912), Ball (1927 outline; 1933 power paper), later Bassler / Joint Venture Qattara studies, and related papers proposed admitting seawater so the drop could drive turbines while evaporation held a level (**established**). A nuclear-excavation canal concept (**213** underground devices in the Plowshare-era proposal; **established**) was examined and not adopted (**established**). Egypt's 2026 review, reported on **12 May 2026**, rejected seawater filling and also judged a desalinate-at-the-rim pipeline scenario not feasible; its findings, stated risks, and sources are under Prior review in §2 (**established**).
- **Hybrid architecture, in three ledgers:** the design is a hybrid, and its energy claims split into three separate accounts. **Thermal:** the plant's condenser cooling water, heat that would otherwise go back to the sea, feeds MED. No steam is taken from the plant, so its safety basis is unchanged (**working assumption**: a design choice). MED's own electricity use is for pumps and auxiliaries. Nuclear waste heat reduces the marginal energy cost of thermal desalination — it is not free: taking the heat has an opportunity cost, needs equipment, and can affect reactor operation (**established** as design principle in IAEA nuclear-desalination guidance). How much usable heat, at what temperature, a chosen reactor here offers is **not found**. Temperature is the catch: low-temperature MED runs at a top brine temperature of about **65–70 °C** (**established**: makers and the literature give about 60–70 °C), while a reactor's condenser cooling water comes out at only about **30–40 °C** (**published** as typical; condensing steam sits about 10–15 °C above the cooling water). So MED takes its feed heat from that cooling water, possibly run a few degrees warmer than is best for power, within the plant's existing cooling-water limits (**working assumption**: the operator would have to allow it). That costs a little generation: published PWR studies find about **0.39–0.45%** less electrical output per °C of warmer cooling water (**published**), so a 2–5 °C rise would cost about **1–2%** of output (derived). An external top-up then raises the heat to MED's 65–70 °C, a lift of about **25–35 K** (derived): heat pumps on midday solar surplus, or other outside energy. Commercial industrial heat pumps reach a COP of about **3.3** on average at a 61 K lift and up to about **5.8** at small lifts (**published**, Arpagaus et al. 2018); a published fit to such data gives about **4–5** at a 25–35 K lift (derived from that fit). This card uses a COP of **3–5** (**working assumption**). It is cheap heat, not free heat. MED on this card runs about **1–2.5 kWh/m³** electrical plus **230–390 kJ/kg** thermal, which is about **64–108 kWh** of heat per m³ (**published-contested**: published ranges vary by plant, design, and vintage; plain MED makers quote under 1 kWh/m³ electrical, MED with thermal vapor compression about 1.5–2.5; the heat figure is converted arithmetic). If heat pumps supplied all of that heat, they would need about **13–36 kWh of electricity per m³** (derived: 64–108 kWh of heat ÷ COP 3–5), roughly 2–12 times RO's 3–5.5 kWh/m³ (derived: 13 ÷ 5.5 ≈ 2.4; 36 ÷ 3 = 12). So a full heat-pump lift costs more electricity than putting the same power into RO. The top-up is a sizing question: how much of MED's heat can come from cheap outside energy, such as solar surplus with no better use, before RO is the better buy (**not modeled**). Hybrid nuclear MED+RO is itself published: an IAEA design example couples a 600 MWth HTGR's 310 MWth of waste heat to LT-MED plus preheated RO for about **2,182 m³/h**, about 52,000 m³/day (**established** as a published design study, not a built plant). That whole design is about 5% of one 1-million-m³/day plant pair (derived), so a large pair needs a much bigger heat and power source; the reactor size for this card is **not found**. **Electrical:** RO runs on surplus electricity when it is abundant — not as a continuous baseload load — at about **3–5.5 kWh/m³** (**published-contested**), alongside pumping and pipe losses. For scale, every 1 million m³/day of RO needs about **125–230 MW** of average electricity (derived: 1,000,000 m³ × 3–5.5 kWh/m³ ÷ 24 h). Because RO runs mainly on surplus power, its installed size must be larger than its average output; that share of hours is **not found**. The solar-clad corridor supplies part of that load where it runs. Illustrative only: a 70 km × 10 m clad strip is 0.7 km², which at 200 W/m² is about **140 MWp** of nameplate (**working assumption**: illustrative inputs, named here — the result is a derived nameplate, not an annual yield — actual output also depends on packing, orientation, and shading — and established inputs do not make the conclusion established). Annual solar yield on a real corridor is **not found**. **Hydraulic:** one illustrative layout is used throughout this card. It was chosen to score the idea; it is not a survey or a locked route (**working assumption**). Heights and areas below are derived from Copernicus GLO-30, a surface model with a few metres of error. Intake and desal sit at a coastal desal and intake site in the vicinity of El Dabaa, using the plant's cooling-water heat piped a few km (illustrative site chosen to score the idea; implies no endorsement by the plant or its owners). The illustrative intake point is about 31.057N 28.501E (**working assumption**). A pipe of about **85 km** carries product water inland. It climbs mostly steadily, with one dip of about **15 m** near km 78, to an upper lake on the plateau (derived). The upper lake site is at about **180 m** and the full lake at about **190 m**. A dam about **3.1 km** long, from a bed at about **171.7 m** to a crest at about **191.7 m**, holds about **14.3 km²** and about **147 million m³** (derived). When full, that is about **3–5 months** of one plant pair's output, or about **2 months** at the full 2.7 million m³/day goal, and only while the lake is not being cycled for storage (derived: 147 million m³ ÷ 1.0–1.5 million m³/day ≈ 98–147 days; ÷ 2.7 million m³/day ≈ 54 days). About 9.5 km to the north-northeast, a natural closed basin fills to about **155 m** with no dam, at about **43.6 km²** and about **0.7 billion m³** (derived). It is a water bank, and only a weak storage pair with the upper lake, at about **56 m** of head (derived). On the descent, a ridge at about **212 m** sits about **4.1 km** south of the upper lake, so the route needs a cut about **20–35 m** deep, about **22–32 m** between a full and a low upper lake (derived). Every energy and storage figure on this card assumes that cut. A booster pump over the ridge instead would cost about **0.09–0.10 kWh/m³** on every cubic metre and wipe out the best case (derived). Past the ridge, the water drops to the cliff foot at about **−71 m**, then runs about **150 km** along the floor to the lake (derived). The lake pools in the southwest pocket centred near 29.35N 26.84E: about **180 km²** at about **−116 m**, about **270 km²** at about **−108 m**, and **490 km²** at about **−97.6 m** (derived). Filling to −60 m would cover about **12,350 km²** (derived). One meter of head is worth about **0.002725 kWh/m³** (E = ρgh, with ρ = 1,000 kg/m³ and g = 9.81 m/s²; **established** as physics). With a lift from sea level to about 180 m and a drop to −97.6 m, the ideal net gain is about **0.266 kWh/m³**, set by the lake depth alone (derived). Real machines lose energy. With a pump-plus-turbine round trip of **80–85%**, split evenly between pump and turbine (**working assumption**), the net is about **0.127–0.165 kWh/m³** before pipe friction (derived). With friction of **0.1–0.4 m per km** (**working assumption**) over the whole route of about 250 km, the result spans about **+0.099 to −0.138 kWh/m³** (derived). Desal, evaporation, and seepage are outside this ledger. In plain words: gravity can roughly pay back its own pumping. At best it leaves about 2–3% of RO's 3–5.5 kWh/m³ (derived), and at worst it is a net cost. It does not pay for desalination. For scale, other systems already pump water far uphill and keep most of that cost, because their water ends up higher than it started: the Colorado River Aqueduct lifts water a combined **1,617 ft** (about 490 m) through five pumping plants at about **2,000 kWh per acre-foot**, roughly 1.6 kWh/m³, and California's State Water Project lifts **1,926 ft** (about 590 m) in one step at the Tehachapi Mountains, with about **3,236 kWh per acre-foot**, roughly 2.6 kWh/m³, from the Delta to the end of its East Branch, net of its own hydro recovery (**published**, other systems, for scale only; m³ conversions derived). Here the water ends up lower, so the climb can be paid back. **Plateau oil and gas:** the plateau between the coast and the depression holds producing oil and gas fields, including Razzak, Alamein, and Abu Gharadig, among others (**published**). How they map against this route is not yet done; it ties into the oil and gas risk the 2026 review named (§2). The real savings come from three other places: reactor waste heat for MED, surplus electricity for RO, and dispatch that runs each machine when its energy is cheapest. Whole-system energy-neutral including desalination is **not claimed**, and gravity cannot deliver it. The test target is low purchased power instead: the plant should buy little or no outside electricity because the corridor's own solar and the reactor's surplus cover the load, which the coupled corridor model has to show; it is not a result. **No measured pump-kWh vs turbine-kWh ratio or pipe loss figure is in this card.**
- **Dispatch and feed warming:** the reactor runs at steady output. Around the solar midday, when grid power is cheap, heat pumps on solar surplus top up more heat for MED; in the evening that power goes to the grid or to RO instead. Using some of the evening hydro release to keep the heat pumps running is an option that is **not modeled**; it is stored midday solar, not extra energy. No steam is diverted from the plant. The upper-lake dam and MED buffering smooth the daily swings. A continuous dispatch optimizer balances electricity, MED, and RO hour by hour, so part of the savings comes from how the plant is run, not only from its hardware (**working assumption**). Spare low-grade heat can also pre-warm the RO feed from about **20 °C** to the low **30s °C** (**working assumption**: intake temperature varies by season). Membrane makers' rule of thumb is about **3%** more water through the membrane per °C (**established**), which here might mean **5–10%** less RO energy (**working assumption**, not modeled). The trade-offs: salt passage rises faster, about **6%** per °C by the same rule of thumb (**established**), and membranes have a feed temperature limit of about **35–45 °C** (45 °C is one major maker's stated limit, **established**; the lower end is a **working assumption** for long membrane life).
- **Lake goal and desal sizing:** the goal is a lake about the size of Lake Tahoe, about **490 km²** (**established** as Tahoe's area). Holding that size steady takes about **2.7 million m³/day** just to replace evaporation (**working assumption**, derived: 490 km² × the 5,500 m³/day per km² rate below), and more if the bed seeps or water is drawn off. For scale, Taweelah RO in Abu Dhabi makes about **909,000 m³/day**, Sorek 2 RO in Israel about **670,000 m³/day** installed, Jubail's MED plant in Saudi Arabia about **800,000 m³/day**, and the Ras Al Khair thermal-plus-RO hybrid about **1,036,000 m³/day** (**established**). One large MED+RO plant pair is taken here as about **1.0–1.5 million m³/day** of average delivered water (**working assumption**, from those reference sizes; installed size would be larger, see Electrical), so the goal needs about **2–3** such pairs (**working assumption**, derived: 2.7 million ÷ 1.0–1.5 million). The first step is one pair and a lake of about **180–270 km²** (**working assumption**, derived: 1.0–1.5 million m³/day ÷ 5,500 m³/day per km², with no seepage and nothing drawn off). For comparison: Shasta Lake is about **120 km²**, Lake Tahoe about **490 km²**, and Lake Mead about **640 km²** at full pool (**established**). The MED vs RO split, brine volume, and reactor thermal and electric rating are still **not found**.
- **Co-siting:** the desal plants sit a few km from the reactor, take its condenser cooling-water heat by pipe, and may share its seawater intake and outfall (**working assumption**). Big plants need hectares, not square kilometers: Sorek 2 sits on about **9.4 ha** (**established**).
- **Hybrid desal pilots (not this plant's design):** NF pretreatment let a **20 m³/day** MSF pilot run at **130 °C** top brine temperature for 1,200 hours without scale additive, with recovery up to **70%** versus about **35%** for conventional MSF (**established** as a published pilot result). A GOR of 13 for that pilot was **not found** — other NF-MSF configurations report different GORs, and published NF energy figures vary (**published-contested**). MED-AD hybrids roughly doubled to tripled distillate in modeled and lab/prototype work by extending the cold end from about **40 °C** toward **5–10 °C** (**published-contested**: modeled and prototype results, not commercial scale). Neither hybrid is a Qattara plant design or cost.
- **Freshwater retention:** whether a filled freshwater surface leaks into saline aquifers, how fast evaporation removes inventory, and whether a useful lake level can be held are **unknown**. Ball's 1933 estimate put depression-lake evaporation at **4–6 mm/day** (common secondary range, Ball 1933, for the seawater scheme). Freshwater evaporates faster than brine: Ball's figures imply about **5.5 mm/day** for freshwater, falling by about **2.7 mm/day** toward near-saturated brine (**published** figures, via a Texas Water Development Board summary of Ball); a measured freshwater-lake evaporation and leakage rate for this basin was **not found**. Pouring into often-salty groundwater as a sweet aquifer bank was considered and **left off** on purpose. Lithium or other mineral grades in the salt pans were **not found** in the studies surveyed, so the pans are not presented as a mineral opportunity; published groundwater salinities in the Moghra aquifer range from brackish near the eastern margins to highly saline toward the depression (**published-contested**: primary studies report widely varying local values); extreme sabkha values were **not found** in a primary source opened here. The retention unknown is practical, not abstract: the floor rests predominantly on the Lower Miocene Moghra Formation — sand, sandstone, and clay layers (**established**) — whose permeability connects to the Moghra aquifer already being pumped for large-scale reclamation northeast of the depression (**established**), so a freshwater surface may not stay fresh or isolated. Salt is the other half of the problem: freshwater sitting on sabkha and salt pans dissolves rock salt from the floor, so the lake grows saltier over time unless the bed is lined or has engineered barriers (**working assumption**: the dissolving is basic chemistry, the rate here is unknown). Evaporation adds to the salt: with no outflow, the lake loses water mainly to evaporation, plus some seepage, and evaporation leaves dissolved salt behind, so the lake gets saltier over time unless some water is bled off, for example to irrigate salt-tolerant crops on its edge (**working assumption**, untested; see Salt management below). Seepage cannot count as bleed-off: it is uncontrolled and puts wells and Siwa at risk. This is the retention mechanism that must be tested. Long-term retention is not modeled on this card (**not found**). The scale of that evaporation is not small: at Ball's **5.5 mm/day** freshwater rate (**published** figure above; a rate measured for this basin was **not found**), each **1 km²** of lake loses about **5,500 m³/day**, and **100 km²** about **550,000 m³/day** (**established** as arithmetic from that rate). What the receiving basin is for — managed reservoir, habitat, agriculture supply, storage, or the lake itself — is an open, scored choice that sets how much of that loss matters; this card does not pick one.
- **Salt management (design response to the retention risk; not tested):** the lake is not kept as pure as its desalinated feed. It is held at about **1,000 mg/L** (0.1%), freshwater by the USGS definition (under about 1,000 mg/L) (**published**: [USGS, Saline Water and Salinity](https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/saline-water-and-salinity) classes fresh water as under 1,000 ppm and slightly saline as 1,000–3,000 ppm; the target itself is a **working assumption**). At that level, water gives full yields of barley, wheat, cotton, sugar beet, sorghum, date palm and tomatoes; maize, citrus, potatoes and grapes lose up to about a tenth of their yield; beans, carrots and onions lose more (derived from FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper 29 irrigation-water thresholds, ECw in dS/m converted to mg/L with ×640; full-yield limits about **3,400 mg/L** barley, **3,260** cotton, **3,000** sugar beet, **2,560** wheat, **1,730** date palm, about 1,090 tomato, 700–830 for maize, citrus and alfalfa). The feed is MED distillate or two-pass RO, at about **10–30 mg/L** (**published**: distillate is normally about 20–30 mg/L, second-pass RO permeate about 5–13 mg/L), plus Nile water in surplus years (see Nile feed in §3). Skipping the second RO pass does not pay: single-pass permeate at about 200–400 mg/L would need about **40%** more desalinated water to hold the same lake (derived), which costs more energy than the second pass's roughly **0.1–0.3 kWh/m³** (**working assumption**: published second-pass figures vary by plant). At steady state a bleed of about **1–12%** of the inflow holds the lake at about 1,000 mg/L (derived; about 1–3% with desal water alone, about 11–12% with about 0.45 km³ a year of Nile water), about **10–135 million m³ a year** (derived, at about 2.7 million m³ a day of evaporation). The bleed waters a zone of about **110 km²** (about **26,000 feddan**) of salt-tolerant farms on the lake edge (**working assumption**: zone size). At steady state the bleed fully waters about 1,200–27,000 feddan of it, so the zone is still about the right size and is fully used in the high-Nile case (derived, at an assumed 5,000–8,000 m³ per feddan a year). In the early years, while pore brine leaks out of the floor, holding 1,000 mg/L would take a bleed of roughly **340 million m³ a year**, more than the zone can use (derived, at an assumed 0.3 million t of salt a year from the floor); the excess would go to the bermed pans, or the lake runs saltier for a while (see pore brine below). The farms' drainage, about 15–25% of what they apply (**working assumption**: a typical leaching fraction), goes to the salt cell, so the cell receives about 4–6 times less water than a direct bleed (derived). **Salt cell:** a natural low salt flat at the lake's northeast end, about **47–54 km²** (derived), closed by a dike about **1 km** long and about **2 m** high with a gravity gate (derived: about 0.8 km, at most about 2.1 m); a reserve pit of about **19–22 km²** and about **127 million m³** to the south (derived); and bermed evaporation pans of up to about **368 km²** for the high-Nile case (derived). Brine is denser than lake water, so the outlet draws off the bottom of the deep sump rather than the surface (**working assumption**). Seepage still cannot count as bleed-off.
- **Initial salt flush (one-time, before the lake is held):** the floor's salt crust is the biggest short-term salt source. Each centimetre of crust over 490 km² holds about **10.6 million t** of salt, which would add about **1,450 mg/L** if mixed into the full lake (derived, assuming solid halite). Its thickness under the lake is **not found**. The plan: sheet water over the crust, collect the brine in the deep stage below about **−120 m** (about 159 km², about 0.9 km³ of room; derived), pump it to the salt cell or the pans, and repeat. A crust of up to about **10 cm** clears in about **2–4 cycles** over about **3–8 months** (derived, in a simple model at 85% brine recovery and about 45 days a cycle, both **working assumptions**). It uses about **0.24–0.75 km³** of flush water in all (derived), from the first Nile-surplus year rather than desalinated water (**working assumption**: a design choice); a small surplus year covers only part of that, so the flush may span two. The flush does not depend on the Nile option: desalinated water can do it instead, taking about 5 months to 2 years of one plant pair's output, or about 3–9 months at the full 2.7 million m³/day goal (derived: 0.24–0.75 km³ ÷ 1.0–1.5 million m³/day ≈ 160–750 days; ÷ 2.7 million m³/day ≈ 89–278 days). Pumping the brine to the cell takes about **55–190 GWh**, once (derived, 25–40 m of lift). The cell, reserve pit and pans have room for the brine and the salt it leaves (derived: 10 cm of crust settles about 1.0–1.4 m thick on the cell). **Pore brine is the biggest unknown:** salty water held in the sediment below the crust keeps diffusing out for years. In a simple model it adds roughly 1,200–3,800 mg/L over the first decade, depending on how fast it diffuses (derived; diffusion rate unknown). So the lake is allowed to run saltier in the early years, roughly **1,500–3,000 mg/L**, with a larger bleed (derived from a simple diffusion model: about 1,300–3,300 mg/L, peaking around years 12–16, with a bleed of about 340 million m³ a year). How long it takes to settle near 1,000 mg/L depends on how deep the floor brine goes (**not found**); in the simple model it is decades, about 30 years in the slow-diffusion case and longer otherwise (derived). Field cores for crust thickness and pore brine come first (§7).

## 1. The problem
Picture a freshwater lake about the size of Lake Tahoe, about 490 km², with a green, planted edge, in a part of the Western Desert that today is salt pans, sabkha, and sand. The first step is smaller: one plant pair holding a lake of about 180–270 km², if evaporation, seepage, and salt allow it.
Arid basins sit below sea level within tens of kilometers of a salt sea. Historical engineering answered that geometry by admitting seawater: turbines on the drop, evaporation holding a level, and a hypersaline lake or salt pan as the end state. That path was rejected for Qattara in the May 2026 review on groundwater salt, soil, habitat, petroleum overlap, and cost grounds. The idea has been officially studied beyond seawater, too: the same review judged desalination options not feasible, including desalinating at the rim and piping the water out, and favored not using the depression as a reservoir at all (§2).
Freshwater is the scarce good inland. Making it at the coast and moving it over an escarpment costs energy. Without recovery on the descent, the lift is a pure loss; with it, most of the lift comes back, but desalination still costs energy. Without a scored brine and intake plan, coastal desal is an unfinished plant.
## 2. The insight
This is worth testing because the bet is not that gravity is free. It is that reactor waste heat for MED, cheap midday surplus power for RO, a solar-clad lift, and hour-by-hour dispatch could make freshwater cheaper than is usually assumed for a project like this, with the drop paying back most of the pumping. The limits come with it: the drop does not pay for desalination, whole-system energy-neutral including desalination is not claimed, and the test target is low purchased power, meaning the plant buys little or no outside electricity, which the coupled corridor model has to show.
Saltwater flooding is out: Egypt's May 2026 Cabinet review rejected filling the depression with Mediterranean seawater. Use the depression as a **freshwater** receiving volume, not as a seawater hydropower sink. Desalinate at the coast as a hybrid: reactor waste heat drives MED (its own electricity goes only to pumps and auxiliaries), and surplus electricity drives RO when it is abundant. Lift through solar-clad pipelines that generate along the corridor. Recover what the drop allows through hydro into the basin. A held freshwater surface is worth scoring against the rejected seawater alternative and against doing nothing.
Qattara is the classic geometry people mean. The site stays whichever basin has the best lift-to-drop, so hydro pays back as much of the pump as possible.
**Prior review:** Egypt has studied this. A ministerial committee formed under decree **3433** of **2016** assessed **five** development scenarios for the depression, and the Cabinet Media Center published the findings on **12 May 2026**. The committee rejected filling the depression with Mediterranean seawater, judged scenarios 1, 2, 3, and 5 not feasible, and favored scenario 4: not using the depression as a water reservoir (one outlet words it as a seawater reservoir). Scenarios 1 to 3 used all or part of the depression to store water and make power, two of them with desalination for farming. Scenario 5 is the closest to this card: desalinate seawater using the height difference at the rim, pipe the water straight to development uses, and use the salts from desalination in industry (**established**; see Appendix). The stated risks, framed mostly around seawater filling, were salty water seeping into groundwater and nearby wells, saltier soil, harm to desert ecosystems including Siwa Oasis, overlap with **35** oil production development areas and **8** exploration areas, relocating oil and gas pipelines, mineral deposits (bentonite, and carbonaceous shale at **6** sites), the high cost of a canal from the sea, and hydropower costing more than other power sources (**established**). Why scenario 5 in particular was judged not feasible is **not found** in the published findings. This card asks a different question: a freshwater lake rather than seawater, reactor waste heat for MED, RO on surplus power, hour-by-hour dispatch, and a basin bed that is lined or tested before any fill. It is still a reservoir in the plain sense the favored scenario avoids, and it still faces the seepage, Siwa, oil and gas, and cost risks. This card does not claim to overturn the review.
**Serious rival:** coastal desalination piped directly to farms and towns, with no depression lift or lake. This proposal differs by spending lift energy, most of it recovered on the descent, to create a held inland freshwater lake instead of only delivering water at the coast.
## 3. The proposal
### Coastal desal
One nuclear-driven hybrid plant on the Mediterranean: MED on reactor waste heat, RO on surplus electricity when it is there. Cooling-water heat is not free — running the cooling water a few degrees warmer costs a little generation, needs equipment, and needs the operator's consent — but it reduces the marginal energy cost of the thermal share. Product water is freshwater. Brine disposal, intake ecology, and plant siting are first-class design, not footnotes. The desal plants sit at a coastal desal and intake site in the vicinity of El Dabaa, using the plant's cooling-water heat piped a few km (illustrative site chosen to score the idea; implies no endorsement by the plant or its owners), and may share the plant's seawater intake and outfall (**working assumption**). The reactor runs steady with no steam taken. An external top-up, mainly heat pumps on midday solar surplus, raises the cooling-water heat to MED temperature, with a dispatch optimizer balancing power, MED, and RO.
### Solar-clad lift
Pipelines carry product water over the escarpment. Solar cladding helps supply the lift and RO load. Pipe diameter and solar fraction are unknown; the illustrative route is in the Lab card above. The upper-lake dam can turn that lift into pumped storage paired with solar: pump water up when solar power is abundant, hold it — perhaps a week (**working assumption**; no source found verifies that duration) — and release it through the descent turbines when power is most needed. Between the upper lake and the cliff foot, about **251–261 m** of head, depending on fill, is worth about **685–710 MWh per million m³** before losses (derived), or about **0.61–0.66 GWh** delivered per million m³ after an 80–85% round trip (**working assumption** for the round trip; derived). At 1–1.5 million m³/day, that is a modest daily shift: about **0.6–1.0 GWh/day** back out for about **0.75–1.2 GWh/day** pumped in (derived). That 80–85% storage round trip is close to the roughly **80%** the US Department of Energy gives for new pumped-storage plants (**published**, US DOE 2023). Before pipe friction, the drop's own net gain at that flow, about **0.13–0.25 GWh/day** (derived: 0.127–0.165 kWh/m³ × 1–1.5 million m³/day), is about the same size as the storage cycle's daily losses of about **0.15–0.2 GWh/day** (derived); with a narrow pipe, friction erases it. The cliff-foot reservoir needs dikes (**working assumption**), and pumping back up also has to clear the 212 m ridge. Draining the full 147 million m³ upper lake would give about **90–97 GWh** delivered (derived), but at that flow it takes months, so it is not a daily figure (derived). A published pumped-storage variant for this depression places an upper site at about **215 m** on the rim, with about **275 m** of drop to a lake at 60 m below sea level (**established** as published proposal figures, Ragheb 2019, written for the seawater scheme; applying them to freshwater flows is a **working assumption**). This card's energy ledger uses its own DEM-derived layout, not these figures. Ridge storage shifts energy in time at a round-trip loss; it does not add energy.
### Hydro into the basin
On the descent into the below-sea-level floor, turbines recover head. The receiving volume is a depression lake of desalinated freshwater — local humidity, agriculture, and wildlife as possible end uses once water quality and retention are shown. Not Mediterranean brine.
### Staging
First build: one reactor-class source, one corridor, one basin. That first step is one large MED+RO plant pair holding a lake of about 180–270 km². The goal is a lake about Lake Tahoe's size, about 490 km², with two to three plant pairs. Wild end-state of a large inland freshwater frontier stays on the shelf as aspiration. Honest measurement starts at corridor scale.
### Nile feed: future option (not part of the scored design)
A later addition to this card; the route is the lowest-energy path found straight to the main lake. Nothing here enters the card's energy, storage, or lake figures above. Heights and lengths in this section are derived from Copernicus GLO-30 and GLO-90, surface models with a few metres of error (vegetation and buildings add metres). Not costed and not surveyed: an idea for a later study.
- **What it is:** in surplus years only, run some Nile water by its own canal straight to the main lake on the depression floor, dropping through a chain of small hydro plants on the way down. It does not use the upper lake or the El Dabaa pipe, so it has no storage: the hydro runs only while the water flows (**working assumption**: a design choice). A Nile feed would use only water that Egypt's own planners judge spare in a given year, after existing users, and would switch off otherwise.
- **Route (route B, the Natrun corridor):** a clean intake at the Delta head near Qanater, upstream of where farm drainage enters the western canals (**working assumption**: a dedicated channel, not shared canal water). The river there carries about **170–240 mg/L** of dissolved salt (**published**: a 2001 monitoring survey measured about 173 mg/L at El-Kanater and about 240 mg/L at the Delta barrage). The canal runs west along the plain north of Wadi El Natrun, on ground at about **15–37 m**, without entering the wadi (derived). It climbs to a saddle of about **110.5 m** northeast of Moghra, near 30.49N 29.51E (derived from GLO-30; GLO-90 gives about 111–114 m), then descends about **208 m** to the main lake at about −97.6 m through about **5** small hydro steps of roughly 40 m each (derived; step count a **working assumption**). Total length about **507 km**, of which about **221 km** is on the pumped side (derived). The lift is about **100–106 m** (derived). Part of the canal crosses the salt flats on embankments, and the lowest-cost alignment is **not surveyed**.
- **Why this route:** every intake from Rosetta to Assiut has to cross the same saddle, about 110.5 m, because the lowest crest between any Nile or canal point and the depression sits there (derived). Direct westward crossings from Fayoum, Minya or Assiut top out at about **150–200 m** (derived). So a far-upstream intake does not lower the crest; it only adds canal length. Lake Nasser is the only Nile water above the crest, and no open-canal gravity route from it was found (derived). Dropping into Wadi El Natrun (floor about **−21 m**) and pumping back out adds lift: about **126 m** out of the wadi and about 0.03–0.04 kWh/m³ lost on the dip (derived), next to hypersaline lakes and a bird area. A gravity-only version would need a tunnel or cut of about **125–130 km** (derived). The shortest route, about **397 km** from the Nubaria canal near Amreya, saves energy but takes canal water mixed with farm drainage (derived; the drainage mix is a **working assumption** from how the western Delta canals are fed).
- **Volume:** about **0.1–0.5 km³ a year**, in surplus years only (**working assumption**). That is about **3–16 m³/s** averaged over a year (derived). Since the 1970s, Nile flow reaching the sea has averaged about **6.2 km³** a year at first, falling to about **1.7 km³** a year by the 1990s (**published**). How often a surplus year happens is **not found**.
- **Energy:** as an open canal, pumping takes about **0.33–0.39 kWh/m³** and the hydro steps return about **0.44–0.49 kWh/m³**, so the net is about **+0.05 to +0.16 kWh/m³**, a small gain (derived; pump and turbine each **89–92%** efficient, about the card's 80–85% round trip, and canal friction **0.05–0.10 m per km**, both **working assumptions**). As a pressure pipe, at **0.1–0.4 m per km** of friction (**working assumption**), it runs about **−0.36 to +0.09 kWh/m³**, so it can be a gain or a cost (derived). For comparison, the shorter Amreya route runs about +0.08 to +0.18 (canal) and −0.23 to +0.12 (pipe) (derived). The real win is that this water needs no desalination, against RO's 3–5.5 kWh/m³. Canal evaporation is not small: a 20 m wide canal of this length loses about **20–25 million m³ a year** (derived, at 2–2.5 m/yr open-water evaporation, a **working assumption**).
- **Wadi El Natrun branch:** a lined branch of about **18–20 km**, taken off after the first lift, reaches the wadi's north rim (derived). With drip irrigation it could water about **8,000–25,000 feddan** (derived from the capped branch volume below at typical drip duties; a **working estimate**). The point is to replace well pumping, not to add farmland: the wadi's aquifer is already overdrawn, with published over-abstraction of about **106 million m³ a year** and water levels down about **5 m** in a decade (**published**). The branch is capped at about **20%** of each year's surplus, so the lake still gets most of the water (**working assumption**). Its drainage must be kept away from the natron lakes and the bird area, with a no-farm buffer of roughly **2.5 km** around the wadi floor (**working assumption**), and the branch avoids the monasteries (on UNESCO's tentative list). Wadi El Natrun is an Important Bird Area (**published**, BirdLife). Honestly, the branch is an energy loss: water lifted to the rim never drops through the hydro steps, which costs roughly **0.1 kWh/m³** of the branch water (derived). Its value is aquifer relief.
- **Salt:** going further upstream does not make the water salt-free: even Lake Nasser water carries roughly 150 mg/L (**working assumption**, from published Nile salinity ranges). Without an outlet, a 0.1–0.5 km³ feed at about 170–240 mg/L would add about **2–16 mg/L a year** to the main lake, which holds about **7.2–7.3 km³** (derived). The lake's bleed-off and salt cell (Salt management, in the working figures above) handle that: with the Nile feed added, the bleed rises from about 1–3% to about **11–12%** of inflow at the 1,000 mg/L target (derived). The salt cell has room for that, except near the full 0.5 km³ a year, which needs the extra bermed pans (derived). Precedent: the Toshka lakes, filled by Nile overflow in the late 1990s, turned salty and their fishery collapsed (**published**).
- **Sediment:** low, about **50 ppm** (**published**, preprint), so silting is a minor concern.
- **Ecology:** farm-drain nutrients could feed algal blooms, which is one reason for the clean Delta-head intake. The snails that spread schistosomiasis have followed Nile water into newly reclaimed desert farmland, and in Nile canals one of them thrives among water hyacinth (**published**, PMC2476091 and PMID 10605512). So the feed needs screening and treatment before the main lake. Bringing Nile life (fish, plankton, plants) into the lake is the hoped-for biodiversity gain (**working assumption**); what screening should let through is untested.
- **Seepage:** a bigger lake, and a long canal, mean more seepage toward wells, groundwater, and Siwa. The canal would need lining where it crosses permeable ground (**working assumption**).
- **Open:** cost; New Delta overlap; a ground survey of the saddle and the embankment sections; how often a surplus year happens; the Natrun branch drainage design; whether the canal's timing lines up with cheap power for its pumps.
### What this is not
- Not flooding Qattara (or any basin) with seawater.
- Not Ball/Bassler seawater hydropower revived under a freshwater name.
- Not a claim that groundwater refill creates a sweet aquifer bank in salty formations.
- Not a measured energy-neutral ratio, and not a claim that the system is energy-neutral.
- Not a locked site. Qattara is the classic candidate.
## 4. Why this
Seawater fill uses the drop but destroys the freshwater use-case and was rejected for Qattara on environmental and petroleum grounds. Coastal desal without hydro recovery wastes the basin’s head. Dry inland solar or wind without a water product does not create a lake. This proposal ties desal, lift, and drop into one scored corridor so the depression’s depth works for freshwater rather than against it.
## 5. How you'd know
After a first-corridor design score (and, if built, after steady product water reaches a held level):
- Pump work, pipe losses, desal specific energy, and turbine recovery are measured on the same water mass; net energy per cubic meter is published
- Product water in the basin stays within a stated salinity and contaminant band
- Leakage to groundwater and evaporation losses are measured, not assumed zero
- Brine and intake impacts at the coast stay inside the plant’s design limits
- Petroleum, settlement, and habitat constraints on the chosen corridor are explicit accepts or refuses — not surprises after fill
Megawatts of desal nameplate with no lift-to-drop score is a weak result.
## 6. First test
One corridor paper-and-field score before any fill commitment. Match plant, pipe, and basin to one candidate depression (Qattara or better lift-to-drop). The first, decisive test is a coupled model; the seven scored things follow it.
1. **Coupled corridor model (decisive, first).** One spreadsheet, hourly over a full year, for a single corridor: MED output from the reactor's usable waste heat (heat temperature, thermal MW available, MED performance, operating hours); RO on surplus electricity only; the dispatch rule (steady reactor with no steam taken; heat-pump top-up for MED on midday solar surplus; more power to the grid or RO in the evening); pipe friction and pump and turbine efficiency on the chosen route; solar annual yield (not nameplate) against annual pumping plus RO consumption, with the storage or grid exchange needed to close the gaps; and a water balance for the receiving basin (evaporation, leakage, make-up flow) at a stated size and stated purpose. Run sensitivity ranges on every input that is a range on this card. If the three ledgers do not close on the same water in the model, nothing else on this list rescues the route.
2. **Lift-to-drop.** Pump kWh, pipe loss, and turbine kWh on the same mass balance. If hydro cannot pay back most of the pump once friction is counted, the route fails this test.
3. **Freshwater, not seawater.** Product is desalinated water. No Mediterranean admit.
4. **Brine and intake.** Disposal path and intake ecology are designed and costed, not deferred.
5. **Retention.** Evaporation and leakage estimates for that basin floor, plus how fast freshwater dissolves salt from the sabkha and salt-pan floor, with and without a liner or barrier; salty-aquifer bank claim stays off unless geology seals or stratifies. Pass only if modeled and field-tested seepage, including salt the lake has dissolved, does not raise salinity in nearby wells or groundwater; fail otherwise. The first field step is cores of the lake floor for crust thickness and pore brine, before any flush or fill.
6. **Site unlock.** Compare at least one alternate basin on lift-to-drop. Qattara wins only if the numbers say so.
7. **Overlap and ecosystems.** Pass only if the corridor and lake footprint avoid, or are cleared to use, the oil production and exploration areas, the oil and gas pipelines, and the mineral sites the 2026 review named, and an ecology study shows no harm to Siwa Oasis, its springs and wetlands, or nearby desert habitat; fail on any one. Settlements (Qara, Siwa adjacency) can still refuse the corridor.
8. **Scale honesty.** First build is one reactor, one corridor, one basin. Inland-sea rhetoric is not the test.

## 8. Why it might not work
- Desal energy dominates, and pipe friction turns the small gravity gain into a net cost, so the lift adds energy on top of desalination and the plant buys far more outside electricity than the low purchased power target allows.
- Freshwater leaks into saline aquifers or evaporates faster than the plant can replace at acceptable cost.
- Freshwater dissolves salt from the floor and the lake turns brackish, or that salty water seeps into groundwater and nearby wells, the first risk the 2026 review named, unless lining or barriers work at a cost the project can carry.
- Pipe friction and pump and turbine losses eat the small net gravity advantage, so hydro only offsets part of the lift.
- Brine or intake ecology fails coastal permitting.
- Oil and gas areas and pipelines, settlements, or harm to Siwa and desert habitat block the only workable pipe route or lake footprint.
- An alternate basin beats Qattara, and Qattara-locked rhetoric fights the better geometry.
- Cost of nuclear desal plus long pressure pipe exceeds alternatives that deliver water without a depression lake.
- The project drifts back into seawater admit under a hydropower excuse — the path already rejected in May 2026.
- Scale jumps to “inland sea” before one corridor is scored.
Wrong in one line: the corridor cannot show lift-to-drop and brine that support a held freshwater basin.
## 9. Stress-test
- **“Near energy-neutral.”** Not claimed. Desalination needs about 1–2.5 kWh/m³ of electricity plus heat for MED and 3–5.5 kWh/m³ for RO, while gravity returns at most about 0.1 kWh/m³ net on the illustrative layout (+0.099 to −0.138 kWh/m³, derived, assuming the ridge cut), so gravity cannot make the system energy-neutral. The test target is low purchased power instead: the plant should buy little or no outside electricity because the corridor's solar and the reactor's surplus cover the load. The coupled corridor model has to show that; if purchased electricity cannot be kept to the surplus the design assumes, the target fails for that design.
- **“A week of ridge storage.”** Working assumption, not a sourced duration. If the crest reservoir is too small to hold useful energy, or evaporation and leakage drain it before the release window, the pumped-storage claim fails and the reservoir is only a surge tank.
- **“Flood Qattara.”** False for this idea. Seawater admit is the historical scheme; May 2026 rejected it. This path is coastal freshwater only.
- **Ball 125–200 MW / Bassler ~5,800 MW.** Secondary accounts put Ball-scale power near **125–200 MW** (**established**, Ball's preliminary calculations) and one Bassler-era concept near **5,800 MW** (base plant plus pumped storage; **published-contested**, published stage figures do not fully agree). Those figures describe the rejected seawater path, not this proposal's power budget. Freshwater recovery here will be smaller: at the same flow and head freshwater yields slightly less power than seawater, and this scheme recovers only on desalinated product flow after paying desal and lift (**working assumption**, derived from the density difference and the cost order above).
- **Evaporation at lake scale.** At Ball's **5.5 mm/day** freshwater rate (**published** figure; measured for this basin **not found**), **100 km²** of held freshwater loses about **550,000 m³/day** to evaporation (**established** as arithmetic from that rate). If make-up flow at the chosen lake size and purpose costs more energy than the corridor saves, the held-lake version fails and only the delivered-water version remains.
- **Groundwater refill as sweet storage.** Left off on purpose. Salty formations do not reliably become a useful freshwater bank.
- **Qattara is required.** False. Classic candidate. Site = best lift-to-drop.
- **19,605 km² is the build.** Basin area below sea level is geography. First build is one corridor, one basin slice — not the whole floor.
- **“Egypt's 2026 review already rejected this.”** Partly true. It rejected seawater filling, judged a rim-desal pipeline scenario (scenario 5) not feasible for reasons not published, and favored not using the depression as a reservoir at all. This design differs in what goes into the basin and how desalination is powered: a freshwater lake, reactor waste heat for MED, RO on surplus power with hour-by-hour dispatch, and a bed lined or tested before fill. Those differences do not clear anything by themselves. The design still has to pass the same risks the review named: seepage into groundwater and wells, harm to Siwa and desert ecosystems, overlap with oil and gas areas and pipelines, and cost. If it cannot, the review's conclusion holds for this design too.
- **“Fill it for sea-level drawdown.”** The rejected seawater scheme in modern dress. Impounding a chosen intermediate, partial-fill volume of **361 km³** of seawater equals about **1 mm** of global sea-level drawdown by ocean-area arithmetic (**established** as arithmetic, from ocean area and impounded volume). The depression's capacity to sea level is roughly **1,200–1,340 km³** (**published-contested** across the Ball-lineage fill table at 1,213 km³ and later DEM estimates at about 1,340 km³), so 361 km³ is a partial volume, not the basin's volume. Egypt's May 2026 review rejected seawater filling. This freshwater proposal's relevant metric is annual freshwater throughput: about 2.7 million m³/day at the Tahoe-size goal, or roughly 1 km³ a year (**working assumption**, derived from the sizing above).
- **“This needs a national corridor.”** No. The goal is a Tahoe-size lake, about 490 km², held by two to three large plant pairs at one reactor site. That is about 2.5% of the 19,605 km² below sea level (**established** as arithmetic). The first step is one pair and about 180–270 km². If one pair cannot hold its lake against evaporation, seepage, and salt, the bigger goal fails with it.
- **“The lake will make it rain.”** Not claimed. A lake this size, with planting at its edge, should raise humidity and dew near the shore and might nudge rain slightly downwind; dry, sinking desert air limits more than that. It is a side effect to measure, not a benefit to count.
