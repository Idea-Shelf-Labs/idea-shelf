# Tulare Smart Solar
**Claim:** Solar arrays floated or raised over returning Tulare Lake water, washed with that water, can help pay for holding more water, with power income covering wash and part of the land and water costs.
**Status:** ready · test specified · lab card · unfinished experiment
**Terms (once):** **Tulare Lake** = the seasonal lake on the old bed in the Tulare Lake Basin; also called Pa’ashi. The name is the water body, not this project. **Float** = arrays on open water or forebays. **Elevated** = arrays over seasonal flood and recharge basins, still usable when the water is thin. **Water-wash** = pumping lake, basin, or a dedicated supply to clean panels. **Edge** = a wet, planted margin around the arrays where people and wildlife can use the water's edge. **First cell** = the first place arrays are built, in the hundreds of acres. No site and no operator yet.
Working figures and unknowns (labeled). Nothing below is a build commitment.
- **Scale:** the first build is **hundreds of acres**, then **thousands** if the first cell pays (**working assumption** for staging). Not the whole lake.
- **The bed still holds water:** in spring **2023** the returned lake peaked at about **113,000 acres** (**established**, *Los Angeles Times* and NASA reporting put the peak at roughly 103,000–114,000 acres). Birds used it heavily — shorebirds, ducks, ibises, and egrets feeding and nesting on the temporary water (**established**, Bay Nature and California Department of Fish and Wildlife reporting, 2023). The same warm, shallow water also brought avian botulism detections and mosquitoes (**established**, CDFW, 2023), so a wet edge is a managed place, not a free benefit.
- **Cover can save water elsewhere, unmeasured here:** published floating-solar studies on reservoirs and ponds report evaporation cuts of roughly **30–60%** under the covered surface, depending on how much water is covered (**published-contested**: measured and modeled results from other sites, none at Tulare). One 2024 study found floating panels about **4 °C** cooler than rooftop panels and about **3.3%** higher output (**published-contested**: single-site result). Neither number is a Tulare number until a cell here measures it.
- **Dust is the local tax:** dust on panels in this valley is the main reason the design washes with water instead of wiping dry. A measured Tulare soiling percent, wash frequency, and wash volume per megawatt were **not found** — the first test measures them instead of borrowing a curve from somewhere else.
- **Money and grid:** power price, payback year, capex, wash operating cost, land price, water-purchase cost, and interconnection cost are all **not found** for a first cell; any co-financing amount or funder for flood control, dust control, habitat, or recreation value is also **not found**. Who can stop the first cell is not mapped: private owners, irrigation districts, groundwater agencies, the county, and the grid queue.
## 1. The problem
Tulare Lake was drained, its rivers were diverted, and the bed was farmed. Wet years still put water back on the bed — about 113,000 acres of it in 2023 — and dry years take it away again. While the water is there, birds find it fast and people come to see it. While it is gone, the bed is farmland and dust.
A dry solar farm beside the lake does nothing about any of that. It competes for farmland, makes power, and leaves the water to come and go on its own. The open question is whether power made on the water itself can pay to keep some of that water — and a usable edge on it — in place on purpose, at a price that beats farming the same acres.
## 2. The insight
Put the arrays on the returning water, and use the power income to hold the water and its edge. Float where the water is deep enough to hold the gear. Raise arrays over basins that fill and empty, so flood and recharge water can still move underneath. Wash the panels with pumped water, because dust here defeats dry wiping. Along the arrays, keep a wet, planted edge — shallow margin where birds feed and people can reach the water — as part of the build, not decoration after it.
What that buys, if it works: water that stays longer than the weather leaves it, an edge that wildlife uses and neighbors can visit, and ground under the arrays still doing water work instead of sitting as gravel. The bet is that power sales, plus any water the cover measurably saves, cover wash and part of the land and water costs — with flood control, dust control, habitat, and recreation value helping to carry the rest where a payer for those values exists (**working assumption**; any co-financing amount or funder is **not found**). The bet fails if wash eats the income, if the arrays block flood or recharge movement, or if the edge cannot stay wet, safe, and permitted.
**Serious rival:** ordinary dry-ground solar on nearby farmland, with the water left to flood agencies and public restoration money. That rival is cheaper to build and proven. This idea differs by putting the arrays on the water to test evaporation savings and cooling in place, and by tying the power income by contract to holding water.
## 3. The proposal
### Arrays matched to the water
Float on open water and forebays that hold depth. Elevate over seasonal flood and recharge basins that fill and empty. If one layout serves a cell and the other does not, build the one that serves it. Forcing floats onto mud, or demanding permanent deep water for raised arrays, abandons the proposal.
### Wash with water
A water-wash loop is part of the plant: pump from the lake, the basin, or a dedicated supply, wash on the schedule the dust sets, and measure volume, cost, and yield saved. Dry wipe-only is not the design for this valley. Where the wash water comes from, who holds the right to pump it, and whether used wash water can return to the edge are decided before megawatts are counted.
### A wet edge, kept on purpose
The edge is shallow, planted margin at the water line, designed for bird feeding and roosting and for people to reach and use. It is kept wet as part of operations. It also carries the known risks of shallow warm water — mosquitoes, bird disease in stagnant water, and birds close to panels — so it is monitored and managed, and narrowed or dried in any stretch where those risks fail their tests. An edge that ends up paved, fenced off, or dry is a failed build, and the record should say so.
### Money assigned to water
Power income first pays wash, then part of the land, water, and debt costs. What it pays toward holding water is assigned by contract or easement — and, where the edge trial passes, part of it to keeping a wet edge — and that assignment is written down before a later owner can strip it out. The rest of the cost has to come from whoever values the flood control, dust control, habitat, and recreation the held water provides; no such payer or amount is named here (**not found**). Wildlife use and public use are part of the value the place creates; they are measured and reported, not sold as power.
### Staging
First, a paper score of one candidate cell (ground, water, flood duty, price, grid). Then a small instrumented pilot on that cell (§6). Then the first build in the hundreds of acres, and thousands only if the first build pays and holds its water. Not the whole lake, and no site or operator is named yet.
## 4. Why this
Dry solar makes power and leaves the lake to chance. Flood control alone moves water away as fast as it safely can. Restoration without income depends on grants that come and go. This proposal ties the three together on the same acres: arrays that earn, water that stays longer, and an edge people and wildlife can use — each measured against its cost, on ground where the lake has already shown it can return.
## 5. How you'd know
After the pilot and, if built, after one dust season and one wet–dry cycle on the first cell:
- Power income covers wash and a stated, material share of land and water costs, and any co-financing for flood control, dust control, habitat, or recreation is a named payer with a stated dollar figure — not an assumed one
- Evaporation or water stage on a covered cell differs from an uncovered neighbor cell in the direction the cover predicts, measured on this bed
- Panel yield under the wash schedule holds up against an infrequently washed comparison, at a measured wash volume per megawatt
- Recharge and flood movement still work under the layout that was built
- The edge stays wet for a stated share of the season, bird use is counted, and mosquito and bird-disease monitoring stay inside the limits the health and wildlife permits set
- Acres held under water are counted as acres out of farming, with a price and a payee
Megawatts installed, with none of those measured, is a weak result.
## 6. First test
Cheap before big. Three steps, each able to kill the idea before the next spends more.
1. **Paper score (weeks, no building).** Pick one candidate cell — a 2007 EPA hydrology report names a central storage cell of **7,550 acres** where the Kings River South Fork meets the Tule (**established** as a published map, not a chosen site) — and price it: land, water, flood duty, wash source and right, power buyer, and interconnection queue position. If no cell shows power covering wash plus a material share of land and water costs, with a credible payer for the rest, on paper, stop.
2. **Instrumented pilot (one dust season, small).** On that cell, float or raise a small test array beside an uncovered control patch of water. Measure evaporation or stage on both, panel soiling and the wash volume that restores yield, and whether flood or recharge water still moves as required. A test raft and instruments are not the hundreds-acre build; they exist to get Tulare numbers for the figures that are **not found** today.
3. **Edge trial (same season).** Keep one stretch of planted edge wet. Count bird use, sample mosquitoes, and watch for bird disease with the wildlife agency's methods. If the edge fails its health or permit limits and cannot be fixed by narrowing or re-wetting, the build drops the edge there and says so.
Only after those three: a first build in the hundreds of acres, scored in the open on the seven things in §5.
## 7. Open questions
- Which piece of ground is the first cell, who owns it, and who operates it?
- What evaporation cut and cooling gain does a cover actually get on shallow, seasonal Tulare water?
- What soiling rate, wash frequency, and wash volume per megawatt hold yield in this dust, and who holds the right to pump that wash water?
- What do land, water, and interconnection cost for a first cell — and does power income cover them plus wash and debt, with a surplus left for holding water?
- What must the contract or easement say so a later owner cannot convert the cell to ordinary solar and drop the water and edge terms?
- Which permits does a kept-wet edge need (health, wildlife, flood), and what monitoring limits come with them?
- Who can stop the first cell: owners, irrigation districts, groundwater agencies, the county, flood operators, or the grid queue?
## 8. Why it might not work
- Power income fails to cover wash plus a material share of land and water costs — or no payer materializes for the flood control, dust control, habitat, and recreation value that is supposed to carry the rest. Either way, holding the water goes unpaid.
- Wash water and labor cost more than the yield they save.
- Flooding is too variable: drawdown strands floating gear in dry years, or flood crews cannot move water around the arrays in wet ones.
- The cover's evaporation saving on this shallow seasonal bed is too small to matter.
- The edge fails: mosquitoes or bird disease breach permit limits, or birds drawn to the edge create a strike or fouling problem at the panels.
- Land, water, or interconnection prices make dry solar, or simply paying for water directly, the better buy.
- Owners, districts, or the grid queue refuse.
- Water rights cannot feed the wash or keep water in the cell through a dry season.
- Scale jumps to the whole lake, or to someone else's big-acre concept, before one cell is scored.
Wrong in one line: the first cell cannot pay to hold its water and keep its edge, or the arrays fight the water they were supposed to fund.
## 9. Stress-test
- **“Generation funds the water.”** Not yet a model. No capex, wash cost, land price, water price, or interconnection cost for a Tulare cell was **found**. The claim survives only as the pilot's question: if power cannot cover wash plus a material share of land and water costs, or no co-financing for the other values materializes, the line is dropped for that design. Any co-financing amount is **not found** today.
- **Water rights and pumping fees.** Real and priced. The Tulare Lake Subbasin was designated probationary on **16 April 2024** (**established**, State Water Board Resolution 2024-0012); pumpers who must report pay **$20 per acre-foot** pumped and **$300 per well** per year (**established**, State Water Board basin page; small domestic and low-volume pumpers are excluded). As of August 2026 the Board is barred from enforcing probation only in the Tri-County Water Authority and Southwest Kings GSA territories while court cases run (**established**, Board basin page). If wash or holding water must be pumped groundwater, those fees and reporting duties belong in the cost stack; if the right to pump cannot be had at all, the design fails before it is built.
- **Evaporation and cooling.** Published floating-solar work reports evaporation cuts around **30–60%** under cover and small cooling gains (about **3.3%** more output in one 2024 study) (**published-contested**: other reservoirs and ponds, varied coverage and methods). This bed is shallow and seasonal; if the pilot's covered-versus-open measurement shows a saving too small to price, the water-saving part of the claim is dropped and the arrays stand or fall as power alone.
- **The wet edge.** The 2023 return showed the value and the risk together: heavy bird use of the temporary lake (**established**), and avian botulism detections in warm stagnant water plus mosquito presence (**established**, CDFW and press reporting, 2023). If monitoring breaches health or wildlife permit limits and re-wetting or narrowing cannot fix it, that stretch of edge is dried and the “nice area” claim shrinks to what remains.
- **Flooding variability.** A lake that peaked near **113,000 acres** in 2023 and is absent in dry years (**established**) will strand floats and drown schedules. The layout that cannot handle the cell's wettest and driest years on record is the wrong layout, whatever it earns in an average year.
- **24,000 acres.** Not the plan. That is John Ennis's unapproved concept, submitted to the state and reported in the *Los Angeles Times* on 3 July 2025, with flood protection for Corcoran, Alpaugh, and Allensworth as its stated first reason. This idea starts in the hundreds of acres.
- **“Floating is required.”** False. Floating is useful where open water holds the gear. Elevated arrays over thin water count, and drawdown stranding is a first-order failure.
- **“Solar restores the lake.”** False as stated. Arrays can pay to hold water that arrives; they do not bring the historic lake back, and the card does not claim they do.
- **Farmland is not free.** Every acre held under water is an acre out of production. If the land and water cannot be priced and paid, there is no site — only a picture.
